## What a good CV does

A strong CV presents your skills, experience and achievements clearly and professionally. It has to work for two readers: a recruiter skimming it in seconds, and an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) parsing it automatically. Everything below serves one of those two readers.

Most actuarial and quant CVs are built from four ingredients:

- **Education, qualifications and certifications:** degrees, exams and exemptions, and any professional certificates
- **Action verbs:** bullets that start with what you did
- **Metrics and specifics:** numbers that show scale and impact
- **Technical skills:** the languages, tools and methods you can actually use

## Choose the right file format

| Format | When to use it |
| --- | --- |
| PDF | Always for submitting. It preserves your formatting exactly. |
| DOCX (Word) | Fine for drafting and editing. Convert to PDF before you send it, unless the employer asks for Word. |
| LaTeX | Ideal for precise, professional formatting. Overleaf is a popular free online editor. |

The graduate and experienced CV templates on this site come in both LaTeX and Word versions.

## Personal information

Include only what an employer needs to contact you and find your work:

- your full name, email address and phone number
- your location (city and country)
- relevant professional links: LinkedIn, GitHub, a portfolio

Leave out your date of birth, marital status and a photograph.

## Work experience

- **Achievements over responsibilities.** "Built an automated lapse-analysis pipeline in Python" beats "responsible for lapse analysis".
- **Relevant technical and analytical skills.** Name the methods and tools in context.
- **Quantified results.** Show your contribution with numbers wherever possible.

A reliable pattern for each bullet:

> **Action verb** + **what you did** + **tool or method** + **measurable result**

For example: "Automated monthly claims reporting in Python and SQL, reducing preparation time by [X]%." Replace the placeholders with your real figures.

## Wording that works

Start bullets with strong action verbs, such as engineered, optimised, designed, built, automated, improved, analysed, validated, led or delivered. Avoid filler words like "hardworking", "passionate" or "team player"; let the bullets demonstrate those qualities.

Include the specifics a technical reader looks for:

- **Tools:** Python, SQL, R, C++, Excel/VBA, NumPy, pandas
- **Techniques:** GLMs, machine learning, time-series models such as GARCH, volatility models such as SABR, reporting standards such as IFRS 17
- **Metrics with outcomes:** for example "RMSE reduced by [X]%" or "profit increased by [Y]%"

Only list techniques you can explain confidently in an interview.

## Formatting for ATS compliance

Avoid:

- tables or text boxes for laying out content
- icons, graphics, colours or logos
- images or photographs

These are safe to use:

- simple bold text and bullet points
- black-and-white PDFs
- standard fonts at consistent sizes

A quick test: copy all the text from your PDF into a plain-text editor. If it reads in the right order with nothing missing or garbled, it should parse. For role-specific keywords, see the ATS-friendly CV guide.

## Length and focus

- Keep it concise. One page is ideal unless you have extensive experience; the usual threshold for a second page is around eight or more years.
- Put the most relevant material first, and tailor the top of the CV to each job description.
- Cut anything that doesn't support the role you're applying for.

## File naming and submission

- Submit your CV as a PDF wherever possible, and never as an image.
- Use a professional file name, such as `Firstname_Lastname_CV.pdf`.

## The bottom line

Your CV should demonstrate both technical capability and analytical thinking. Present your achievements and skills concisely, with a clear structure, so that recruiters and automated systems can quickly see that you fit the role.
