## You don't need an actuarial degree

Many actuaries start in other quantitative fields, such as mathematics, statistics, physics, engineering, computer science or finance. The profession values exactly the skills those degrees build: mathematical maturity, comfort with data and the ability to model a messy problem.

What changes for a non-traditional entrant is the route. You usually have fewer exemptions to claim, so you pass more exams yourself, and you need to show employers that your skills transfer.

This guide sets out the steps. For the full structure of each qualification route, see Route to Qualifying as an Actuary.

## Step 1: Choose your professional body

- **IFoA (UK and international).** The IFoA route is very accessible to students with a technical background. Almost any strong quantitative degree is a valid starting point, and you register as a student member directly.
- **ASSA (South Africa).** Most South African students enter through an accredited actuarial science degree, which brings exemptions from the A-series exams. Students from non-traditional degrees can also enter, but they may need strong marks and support from their university. Check ASSA's current student membership criteria before you apply.

If you plan to work in South Africa, check which body your target employers expect and support. Then confirm the current entry rules on the [ASSA](https://www.actuarialsociety.org.za) or [IFoA](https://actuaries.org.uk) website.

## Step 2: Register and check your exemptions

Join as a student member as early as you can. Even without an accredited degree, ask whether any of your previous study qualifies for exemptions. The rules are set by the professional body, and they change, so get a definitive answer rather than assuming either way.

## Step 3: Start with the exams that suit your background

Your background usually gives you a head start on the mathematical foundation subjects: statistics and probability, and financial and actuarial mathematics. Many career-changers begin there to build momentum. The business and economics subjects tend to be newer ground for science and engineering graduates, so budget extra time for them.

A few practical habits help:

- Sit one or two exams per session rather than overloading yourself.
- Use past papers early. The practice question banks on this site are a good place to start.
- Treat your study plan as a multi-year project.

## Step 4: Build practical, visible skills

Employers want evidence that you can do actuarial work, not just pass exams:

- **Excel** is still universal in actuarial teams, so be fluent.
- **Python, R and SQL** are increasingly expected. Practise them on actuarial-style problems.
- **Portfolio projects** turn theory into something you can show. A reserving engine, a pricing model or a stochastic simulation gives you concrete CV bullets and interview stories. The portfolio projects on this site are designed for this.

## Step 5: Get your first actuarial role

You build the qualification's experience requirement on the job, so your first role matters. The ASSA calls this requirement Work-Based Learning; the IFoA sets its own work-based experience requirement. Routes in include:

- graduate programmes, many of which accept a range of quantitative degrees
- vacation work and insight programmes
- analyst roles in pricing, reserving, risk or data teams that sit alongside actuaries

The Actuarial Careers guide lists programmes and employers in South Africa, with typical application timing.

## Step 6: Present your background as an asset

On your CV and in interviews:

- **Translate your experience.** Say "built and validated statistical models in Python" rather than describing a physics lab project in its own jargon.
- **Lead with relevant evidence.** Put exams passed, portfolio projects and programming skills high on the page.
- **Use the right keywords** for the roles you target. The ATS keyword cheat sheet and the CV templates on this site show how.
- **Explain your motivation briefly and positively:** why actuarial work, and why now.

## Step 7: Consider a complementary certification later

Once you are established, a certification such as the CFA, FRM or FMVA can support a move into investments, risk or corporate finance. It doesn't replace the actuarial exams, so prioritise those first. See Certifications for Actuaries for a comparison.
